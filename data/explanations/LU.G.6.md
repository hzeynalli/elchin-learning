# Subject–verb agreement; pronoun–antecedent agreement; run-ons and fragments (LU.G.6, L.4.1 review)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. Subject–verb agreement: one thing → verb with -s (the cat sleeps); more than one → no -s (the cats sleep). "Is/was" for one, "are/were" for many.
2. Pronoun agreement: a pronoun matches the noun it stands for: Leyla → her; the boys → their; each (one at a time) → his or her.
3. A complete sentence needs a subject and a verb and must make sense on its own. "Running down the hill." is a fragment.
4. A run-on joins two complete sentences without the right glue: "I finished my homework, then I went outside." Fix with a full stop, a comma + and/but/so, or a joining word like because/after.
5. Tricky subjects: neither/nor → agree with the nearer noun; a team, a class → one thing (is, its).

## Worked example
"Neither the teacher nor the students ___ ready." The noun nearest the verb is "students" (many) → were. "The team is practising ___ new song" → team is one thing → its. "The match started late it was raining the players were cold" → three sentences: "The match started late because it was raining, and the players were cold."

## Faded example (he completes)
"Each of the boys brought ___ own lunch." Each = one at a time → ___ . "The cats ___ sleeping" → ___ . Fragment or sentence: "Because it was late." → ___ . (Answers: his; are; fragment.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Matching the verb to the nearest noun instead of the subject ("The box of pens are…"). Scaffold: "What is doing the action? Box. One box → is."
- "Their" with "each/every". Scaffold: "Each means one at a time."
- Comma splices. Scaffold: "Two full sentences? A comma alone can't hold them."
- Calling a long fragment a sentence because it is long. Scaffold: "Who is doing what? Does it make sense alone?"

## Representation sequence
1. Words: the three questions — one or many? who does the pronoun stand for? complete or not?
2. Picture: circle the subject, draw an arrow to its verb; draw an arrow from each pronoun back to its noun.
3. Table: subject | one/many | verb.
4. Story: he fixes a short paragraph with one of each error.
5. Teach it: he explains a run-on to a younger child with two toy cars that need a hook (the joining word).

## Check by explaining back
"How do you decide between 'is' and 'are'? How do you fix a run-on?"
