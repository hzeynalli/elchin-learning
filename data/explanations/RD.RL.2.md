# Determine theme from details (how characters respond to challenges); summarise (RD.RL.2, RL.5.2)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. A topic is one or two words (friendship, honesty). A theme is a full sentence about life that the story shows (helping a friend can matter more than winning).
2. To find the theme, look at the challenge the main character faces and how they respond — and what happens because of that response.
3. Ask: what did the character learn, or what did the story show us? Say it as a sentence that would still be true in another story.
4. A summary is short: who, wanted what, what got in the way, what happened. No small details, no opinions.
5. The theme is never stated as "the story is about a goat" — that is the topic.

## Worked example
The Lantern Race: challenge — Sabina's candle goes out. Response — Kamran relights it and gives up his chance; Sabina refuses to leave him. Result — they finish last but share a prize she treasures. Theme: helping a friend can matter more than winning. Summary: Sabina and Kamran run a lantern race; when her flame dies, he relights it and they finish together, last but happy.

## Faded example (he completes)
The Ant Who Saved the Summer. Challenge: winter comes and Tofiq has ___ . Response: he ___ and Nara ___ . Theme: ___ . (Answers: no food; admits he was wrong / asks for help; forgives him but makes him promise to work; prepare in good times for hard times.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Giving the topic as the theme ("it's about friendship"). Scaffold: "What does the story say ABOUT friendship? Make it a sentence."
- Retelling the whole story as a summary. Scaffold: "Four parts only: who, wanted, but, so."
- A theme that is a detail ("goats fall in rivers"). Scaffold: "Would this sentence be true in a different story too?"
- Choosing a moral the text does not support. Scaffold: "Which event shows that?"

## Representation sequence
1. Words: topic vs theme — one word vs one sentence.
2. Picture: a bridge — challenge on one side, response in the middle, lesson on the other.
3. Table: character | challenge | response | result | theme.
4. Story: two different stories with the same theme — what do they share?
5. Teach it: he explains to a younger child why "friendship" is not a theme.

## Check by explaining back
"Tell me the theme of the story we read, and which event proves it."
