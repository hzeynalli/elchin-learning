# Orient the reader: situation, narrator, characters; natural event sequence (LU.W.N1, W.5.3a)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. The opening of a story answers three questions quickly: who is here, where and when are we, and what is going on?
2. Choose a narrator and keep it: first person (I) or third person (he/she/they).
3. Show the situation through action or detail, not by announcing it: "Murad checked his watch again" beats "Murad was worried about being late."
4. Events go in a natural order; each one leads to the next. Plan them as a list before writing.
5. One small problem in the first few sentences gives the reader a reason to keep reading.

## Worked example
"On a windy morning in Qala, ten-year-old Sabina waited on the beach for the race to begin." Who: Sabina, 10. Where/when: Qala beach, a windy morning. What: a race is about to start; the wind hints at trouble. Third-person narrator. Next events: the race starts → the wind blows out her candle → Kamran arrives.

## Faded example (he completes)
Write two opening sentences: sentence 1 names a character and a place (___); sentence 2 shows a small problem through an action (___). Then list three events in order: 1 ___ 2 ___ 3 ___.

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Starting with "It was a normal day" or "Hi, my name is". Scaffold: "Start where something is about to happen."
- Switching narrator half-way (I → he). Scaffold: "Who is telling this? Keep the same voice."
- Events out of order (finding the key before losing it). Scaffold: "Number the events first."
- Telling the problem ("the problem was…"). Scaffold: "Show me the problem through what the character does."

## Representation sequence
1. Words: who, where/when, what — the three questions.
2. Picture: a story map — setting box, character box, problem box, arrow of events.
3. Table: event | what the reader learns | what it leads to.
4. Story: he rewrites a flat opening ("It was a day") as a scene.
5. Teach it: he explains to a younger child why a story needs a "who and where" at the start.

## Check by explaining back
"What must the first few sentences of a story tell the reader, and how can you show a problem without saying 'the problem was'?"
