# Multi-step word problems with all four operations; interpret remainders (G4.OA.2, 4.OA.A.3)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. Read the question last sentence first: what exactly is being asked? Underline it.
2. Find the numbers that matter and what each one is. Cross out numbers that do not help.
3. Plan the steps in words before calculating: "first total, then given out, then left over".
4. Do one step at a time, label each result ("total = 360").
5. Estimate the final answer and check it makes sense for the story (pencils cannot be negative or a fraction).
6. Remainders: decide whether to round up (buses needed), round down (full boxes), or use the remainder itself (left over).

## Worked example
A box holds 24 pencils. A school buys 15 boxes and gives 9 pencils to each of 32 students. How many are left? Ask: left over. Step 1: total = 24 × 15 = 360. Step 2: given out = 9 × 32 = 288. Step 3: left = 360 − 288 = 72. Estimate: 25 × 15 ≈ 375, 9 × 30 ≈ 270, about 100 left. 72 is reasonable.

## Faded example (he completes)
Aysel buys 3 packs of 8 pens and 5 loose pens. Step 1: 3 × 8 = ___ . Step 2: ___ + 5 = ___ . (Answers: 24; 24 + 5 = 29.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Using every number in the story, including the distractor. Scaffold: "What does this number tell us? Do we need it to answer the question?"
- Doing one step and stopping. Scaffold: "Have you answered the question in the last sentence?"
- Picking the operation from a keyword ("more" → add) instead of the situation. Scaffold: "Act it out — is something being joined, taken away, shared or repeated?"
- Reporting a remainder without deciding what it means. Scaffold: "Can half a bus exist?"

## Representation sequence
1. Words: the four questions — what is asked, what do I know, what is the plan, is it reasonable.
2. Picture: bar model — a long bar for the total, shaded for the part given out.
3. Table: step | calculation | result | what it means.
4. Story: he retells the problem in his own words with the numbers in the right roles.
5. Teach it: he writes a two-step problem with one distractor number for the coach to solve.

## Check by explaining back
"Which number in that problem was not needed, and how did you know?"
