# Narrator's or speaker's point of view and how it shapes events (RD.RL.6, RL.5.6)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. The narrator is the voice telling the story. Ask: is the narrator a character (first person: I, me, we) or outside the story (third person: he, she, they)?
2. A first-person narrator only knows their own thoughts and what they see; we trust or doubt them as we would a friend telling a story.
3. A third-person narrator can follow one character closely (we know Rasim's burning face) or several.
4. Point of view shapes what we learn and how we feel: if the story followed Master Farid, we would know his plan and feel less worried.
5. To answer questions: name the narrator type, then say what that lets us know — and what it hides.

## Worked example
The Baker's Apprentice is told in the third person but follows Rasim: we know his face burns and he wants to hide the loaf. We do not know what Farid is thinking, so Farid's laugh is a surprise to us just as it is to Rasim. That surprise is the point of the story.

## Faded example (he completes)
The Crow and the Water Jar. Narrator: first or third person? ___ . Whose feelings do we know? ___ . What would change if the sparrow told the story? We would ___ . (Answers: third; the crow's; only see the crow from outside and not know her thinking / plan.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Saying the narrator is "the author". Scaffold: "The author writes; the narrator is the voice inside the story. Are they the same person here?"
- Confusing a character who speaks a lot with the narrator. Scaffold: "Who says 'I'? Who tells us what happened?"
- Answering only "third person" without the effect. Scaffold: "And so what do we get to know that the characters don't?"
- Thinking the narrator's view is always right. Scaffold: "Could a first-person narrator be mistaken?"

## Representation sequence
1. Words: the pronoun test (I/we vs he/she/they).
2. Picture: a camera — where is it standing? inside one head, or above the scene?
3. Table: narrator type | what we know | what we don't | effect.
4. Story: retell one scene from another character's point of view.
5. Teach it: he explains to a younger child how the same event sounds different told by the black goat.

## Check by explaining back
"How would the lantern story change if Kamran told it?"
