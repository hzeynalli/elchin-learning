# Read tables and graphs; identify variables; plan a fair test; draw conclusions from data (SC.PRAC.1, NGSS practices)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. A table has a heading for each column: read the headings first, then the numbers. A graph has axes with labels and units: read those first too.
2. A pattern is what the numbers do as you move along: up, down, steady, or up-then-down.
3. In an experiment there is one thing you change on purpose (the independent variable), one thing you measure (the dependent variable), and everything else you keep the same (controls).
4. A fair test changes only one thing. If two things change, you cannot tell which one caused the result.
5. A conclusion says what the data shows — no more. "Ice melted fastest in the sun" is supported; "the sun makes everything melt" is not.
6. Repeating a test makes the result more reliable.

## Worked example
Table: ramp height 10 cm → car rolls 40 cm; 20 cm → 80 cm; 30 cm → 120 cm. Headings: height, distance. Pattern: every extra 10 cm of height adds 40 cm of distance — distance goes up steadily. Variables: changed = height; measured = distance; kept the same = the car, the floor, the release. Conclusion: a higher ramp makes the car roll further. Prediction: 40 cm → about 160 cm.

## Faded example (he completes)
Sugar dissolving: hot water 20 s, warm 45 s, cold 120 s. Changed: ___ . Measured: ___ . Kept the same: ___, ___ . Conclusion: sugar dissolves faster when ___ . (Answers: temperature; time to dissolve; amount of sugar, amount of water, stirring; the water is hotter.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Reading a number without its heading or unit. Scaffold: "40 what? Which column?"
- Changing two things "to make it faster". Scaffold: "Then which one did it?"
- A conclusion bigger than the data. Scaffold: "Point to the numbers that prove that sentence."
- Confusing what you change with what you measure. Scaffold: "Which one did YOU decide before starting?"

## Representation sequence
1. Words: change one, measure one, keep the rest.
2. Picture: sketch the graph from the table; draw the trend with a finger.
3. Table: variable | changed / measured / kept same.
4. Story: a fair test of two footballs — he designs it.
5. Teach it: he explains to a younger child why a race with different starting lines is unfair — and links it to fair tests.

## Check by explaining back
"What makes a test fair, and what can a conclusion say?"
