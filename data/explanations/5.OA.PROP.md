# Properties of operations: commutative, associative, distributive, identity (5.OA.PROP, Go Math 1.3)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. Commutative: swap the order, same answer. 7 × 5 = 5 × 7. Works for + and ×, not for − or ÷.
2. Associative: change the grouping, same answer. (7 × 5) × 2 = 7 × (5 × 2). The numbers stay in order; only the brackets move.
3. Distributive: split one number, multiply each part, then add. 6 × 48 = 6 × 40 + 6 × 8 = 240 + 48 = 288.
4. Identity: × 1 or + 0 leaves a number unchanged.
5. Properties are tools: use them to make hard calculations easy, and to check answers.

## Worked example
6 × 48 with the distributive property. Split 48 into 40 + 8 (tens and ones). 6 × 40 = 240. 6 × 8 = 48. Add: 240 + 48 = 288. Check by estimate: 6 × 50 = 300, so 288 is reasonable.

## Faded example (he completes)
(7 × 5) × 2 = 7 × (___ × 2). Which property? ___ . Then 4 × 27 = 4 × 20 + 4 × ___ = 80 + ___ = ___ . (Answers: 5; associative; 7; 28; 108.)

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- Calling every property "commutative". Scaffold: "Did the ORDER change, or the GROUPING, or did a number get SPLIT?"
- Distributing to only one part: 6 × 48 = 6 × 40 + 8. Scaffold: "Both parts of 48 need the 6."
- Thinking subtraction is commutative (8 − 3 = 3 − 8). Scaffold: test it with numbers.
- Splitting 48 into 4 + 8. Scaffold: "48 is 40 and 8, not 4 and 8."

## Representation sequence
1. Words: three questions — order? grouping? splitting?
2. Picture: area model for the distributive property — a 6 × 48 rectangle cut into 6 × 40 and 6 × 8.
3. Table: property | rule | example.
4. Story: 6 friends each buy 48 stickers — think of the packets of 40 and the loose 8.
5. Teach it: he invents one example of each property and names it.

## Check by explaining back
"Show me two different ways to work out 5 × 36 and tell me which property each one uses."
