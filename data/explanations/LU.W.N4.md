# A conclusion that follows from the events (LU.W.N4, W.5.3e)

## The idea (in small steps)
1. The ending must grow out of what happened; it cannot come from nowhere.
2. Three strong ways to end: show how the character has changed; return to an image or line from the beginning; let the character reflect briefly on what happened.
3. Resolve the problem you set up — found, not found, fixed, accepted — the reader needs to know.
4. Show the change through action or a small detail rather than announcing "I learned that…".
5. Stop at the right moment: one or two sentences after the resolution.

## Worked example
Beginning: "The old clock on the wall had not ticked in years." Ending: "As Nigar wound the last spring, the old clock ticked once, then again, and the house seemed to breathe." The ending returns to the clock, resolves the problem, and shows the change without explaining it.

## Faded example (he completes)
Rasim promised to water his grandmother's garden and forgot for three days. Write a two-sentence ending: sentence 1 shows what he does about it (___); sentence 2 shows what has changed in him (___).

## Anticipated errors (and the scaffold)
- "The end" or "then I woke up". Scaffold: "What happened to the problem?"
- A moral tacked on ("I learned that honesty is good"). Scaffold: "Show me the lesson in what he does next."
- An ending about something new (a dragon). Scaffold: "Which thread from the story does this tie up?"
- Ending too early or dragging on. Scaffold: "Find the last strong image and stop there."

## Representation sequence
1. Words: resolve, show change, echo the beginning.
2. Picture: a circle — the ending touches the start.
3. Table: problem | resolution | change shown by…
4. Story: he writes two different endings for the same story and chooses the stronger one.
5. Teach it: he explains to a younger child why "and then a dragon came" is a bad ending for a story about a lost key.

## Check by explaining back
"What are two ways to end a story so that it feels finished?"
